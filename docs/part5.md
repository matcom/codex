# Graphs {.part}

The trie at the end of Part IV was already a graph. We followed the edges of a tree-with-failure-links to scan a text in a single pass, and the structure that made the algorithm work was just a set of nodes with directed edges between them. Part V lets the edges go anywhere — no requirement that the structure be a tree, no constraint that the relations point only at children. Graphs are what you reach for when the structure is *any relation on any set of things*, and the rest of this part is about what that level of generality buys you and what it costs.

The eight chapters move from the most basic question — *how do I even store one of these?* — through traversal, shortest paths, spanning trees, ordering, and finally to flow.

* **Representations of graphs** (chapter 31) sets the substrate. Adjacency list, adjacency matrix, edge list — three encodings of the same object, each cheap for different questions. The choice runs through every later chapter.
* **Breadth-first traversal** (chapter 32) is the simplest graph algorithm. It introduces the *layered frontier* — the set of vertices at distance $k$ from the source — and as a side effect it solves shortest paths in unweighted graphs.
* **Depth-first traversal** (chapter 33) is the swiss-army knife. The same recursion that walks the graph also exposes its structure: connected components, cycles, the discovery and finish times that later chapters lean on heavily.
* **Dijkstra's algorithm** (chapter 34) tackles weighted shortest paths under the restriction that all edge weights are non-negative. The chapter is also where the heap from chapter 19 finally earns its keep.
* **Relaxation under negative weights** (chapter 35) lifts that restriction. Bellman-Ford handles negative edges and detects negative cycles; Floyd-Warshall pushes the same idea to all-pairs. Both are *relaxation* algorithms, and the chapter makes the unifying pattern explicit.
* **Minimum spanning trees** (chapter 36) leans on union-find from chapter 14 and the heap from chapter 19. Kruskal and Prim are two reading orders of the same proof — the **cut property** — and the chapter develops both.
* **Topological sort and strong components** (chapter 37) returns to DFS and squeezes more out of it. Kahn's algorithm orders the vertices of a DAG; Tarjan's algorithm finds the strongly connected components in a single DFS pass.
* **Flow networks** (chapter 38) closes the part with the deepest result. Ford-Fulkerson augments paths until no augmenting path remains, Edmonds-Karp shows that picking the shortest augmenting path bounds the running time, and the max-flow min-cut theorem connects the algorithmic question to its LP-dual.

Three patterns recur through the part. **Representation shapes cost** — the adjacency list vs. matrix choice isn't storage hygiene, it determines which questions are $O(1)$ and which are $O(n)$. **Relaxation unifies the shortest-path family** — Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford, Floyd-Warshall, and Prim's MST are all maintaining an upper-bound estimate and repeatedly tightening it; the difference is the order they pick edges. And **DFS is the lens for structural questions** — connectivity, cycles, ordering, components, bridges — every one of them is DFS plus a small bookkeeping tweak.

Part V leaves three families of graph problems for later parts. **Matching and coloring** are decision problems whose hard cases are NP-complete; they fit naturally in Part VIII. **PageRank, random walks, and community detection** are applied; they belong in Part VII's specialized domains. And **dynamic programming on graphs** — longest path on a DAG, vertex cover on a tree, the SCC-condensation reduction — gets its own treatment in Part VI, building on the structures developed here.
