# Lessons from Part IV {.unnumbered}

Eight chapters about strings, and almost none of the cleverness lived in the algorithms themselves. The cleverness lived in *representation*. Every chapter from 24 onward was the same conceptual object — a way of answering the question "have I matched a prefix of the pattern against a recent suffix of the text?" — stored in a different shape with a different access pattern. Knuth-Morris-Pratt is a deterministic automaton compressed to one integer per state. Boyer-Moore is two tables that encode the same question read in the opposite scanning direction. Rabin-Karp is the same question filtered through a rolling fingerprint. Aho-Corasick is the same question generalized from one pattern to a set, with the failure function reshaped into a tree. Suffix arrays flip the entire substrate — preprocess the *text* instead of the pattern — and answer the question for arbitrary future patterns instead of just one. Edit distance closes the part by changing what the question even means. The algorithms got their names from their inventors, but their shapes are mostly variations on a few ideas.

## The patterns that kept showing up

**Preprocessing-versus-query as an organizing axis.** Every chapter sat at a different point on the same trade-off curve. The brute force in chapter 23 did zero preprocessing and paid for it with an $O(nm)$ worst case. The DFA in chapter 24 preprocessed the pattern into a $\Theta(m \cdot |\Sigma|)$ table and bought $\Theta(n)$ scanning. KMP compressed the same preprocessing to $\Theta(m)$ with the same scan time. Boyer-Moore preprocessed two tables but earned sublinear best-case scanning by reading windows right-to-left. Rabin-Karp preprocessed the pattern's hash and rolled a fingerprint forward in $O(1)$ per shift. Aho-Corasick preprocessed a set of patterns into a single trie-with-failure-links and answered multi-pattern queries in one scan. Suffix arrays moved the preprocessing entirely onto the text, which lets you reuse the index across arbitrarily many future pattern queries. Edit distance preprocessed nothing and accepted $O(nm)$ on every call because the question itself doesn't admit shortcuts. The chapters together are a map of the trade-off — every point on the preprocessing-versus-query plane is occupied by some chapter, and choosing among them is an engineering question about the workload, not an open theoretical question.

**Automata as the universal lens.** The deterministic finite automaton from chapter 24 is the conceptual ancestor of half the part. KMP's failure function is the same automaton with sparse storage — chapter 25's punchline reconstructed every cell of the DFA from the failure pointers alone, proving the compression is lossless. Aho-Corasick is the same automaton lifted from a single pattern to a set, with the failure function generalized from a one-dimensional array to a tree. Even Boyer-Moore's good-suffix rule has an automaton-shaped formal explanation if you squint at the precomputed shift table the right way. Pattern matching *is* automaton design; the choice of algorithm is mostly a choice of how to compress the automaton.

**Randomization as a cost-shape strategy.** The rolling hash in chapter 27 is the treap argument applied to strings. The adversary controls the input — the text, the patterns, the timing — but you control the hash function's parameters, and a random choice of modulus makes the worst case statistically unreachable. The pattern will recur in Part IX (skip lists, randomized incremental algorithms) and in cryptography wherever someone needs guarantees against an adversary they can't see. When you find yourself worrying about a worst case an attacker could construct, ask whether you can make the worst case depend on a coin you flip rather than on the input the attacker picks.

**Indexing the input shifts what's expensive.** Chapters 23 through 28 all preprocessed the *pattern* and scanned the text. Chapter 29 broke that — it preprocessed the *text* into a suffix array and answered an arbitrary pattern query in $O(m \log n)$. The flip is one of the deepest moves in algorithm design. Database indexes preprocess the rows; inverted indexes in search engines preprocess the documents; spatial indexes in computational geometry preprocess the points. The query side gets cheap because the preprocessing did the work once. It shows up again in Part VIII when geometric algorithms preprocess point sets into k-d trees.

**Restriction is power.** Hamming distance only works on equal-length strings, but inside that restriction it's a single $O(n)$ scan — no table, no allocation. Levenshtein generalizes to arbitrary string pairs and pays $O(nm)$ for the privilege. Aho-Corasick's restriction to *fixed strings* is what lets it match thousands of patterns in one pass; extending it to regex would be PCRE territory, an entirely different and much heavier engine. Boyer-Moore's restriction to *exact match* is what gives it the bad-character rule. Every chapter in the part bought its cost shape by accepting a restriction; lifting the restriction would lose the win. Discipline buys cost shape, and the deeper into the book you go the more often the right answer to a performance question is "what restriction can I accept that would let me deliver the shape I want?"

## Meta-lessons

**The obvious algorithm is the baseline you have to beat.** The brute force in chapter 23 was the algorithm with no clever ideas, and yet it's what production code most often ships. CPython's `str.find`, GNU `memmem`, Rust's `str::find` — all of them are brute force with a small skip table on top, not KMP or Boyer-Moore or any of the cleverer algorithms. The reason is that brute force has a tight inner loop with no branches, which runs faster on modern hardware than any algorithm whose inner loop consults a precomputed table. Asymptotic optimality is a different question from production-code optimality, and when the average case is benign the brute force usually wins on constants.

**Representation can collapse cost.** Chapter 25's KMP stored the DFA from chapter 24 in one integer per state instead of one entry per state-character pair, and the scan ran in the same asymptotic time on much less memory. Chapter 29's suffix array stored an index over every substring of a text in $O(n)$ space, where a suffix tree storing the same information would have used $O(n \cdot |\Sigma|)$. The same phenomenon appears in Part V when sparse graphs use adjacency lists instead of adjacency matrices, and in Part VII when persistent data structures store version histories in $O(\log n)$ space instead of $O(n)$ per version. When you see a table whose entries collapse along an axis, ask whether a sparser encoding stores the same information with less overhead.

**Scanning order is a design choice, not a default.** Boyer-Moore made the case starkly. The exact same window of text, examined right-to-left instead of left-to-right, exposed a class of skips that left-to-right scanning structurally could not see. The choice determined whether the cost shape was $\Theta(n)$ or $\Theta(n / m)$. The pattern recurs whenever an algorithm consumes a window of input: the in-order traversal of a BST gives you sorted output; the pre-order traversal gives you serialization; the post-order traversal gives you bottom-up dependency satisfaction. Different orders expose different invariants.

**Two conservative bounds combine by taking the max.** Boyer-Moore's bad-character rule and good-suffix rule are independently correct and independently motivated. The matcher takes the maximum of both shifts on every mismatch, and the result is at least as fast as either heuristic alone on every input. The pattern recurs in branch-and-bound search (combine multiple admissible heuristics, take the max), in interval scheduling with multiple constraints (combine bounds, take the max), in linear programming relaxations (combine valid inequalities, intersect feasible regions). When you have two correct conservative estimates of the same quantity, their maximum is itself correct and usually strictly tighter than either one alone.

**The same DP grid answers a family of related questions.** Chapter 30's edit-distance grid was structurally identical to its longest-common-subsequence and longest-common-substring grids — the cells, the indexing, the bottom-up fill order. What changed was the per-cell recurrence and the post-fill backtracking. That observation is the seed of dynamic programming proper. DP isn't a single algorithm — it's a framework for problems with optimal substructure and overlapping subproblems, and the same grid-of-cells machinery handles dozens of distinct questions by varying the recurrence. Part VI develops the pattern.

## What's next

Part V is a change of substrate. The next eight chapters drop the assumption that the data being walked is a one-dimensional sequence of characters and let the structure become a *graph* — nodes connected by edges, with no implicit left-or-right order, with cycles and multiple paths and arbitrary connectivity. Chapter 28's Aho-Corasick was already half a graph — a trie with failure links is a graph if you draw the failure edges as edges — and chapter 20's trie was a tree, which is a graph with no cycles. The leap into Part V is just letting the edges go anywhere.

The arc starts with the substrate. Graph representations — adjacency lists, adjacency matrices, edge lists, implicit graphs — name the trade-offs. DFS and BFS are the universal traversals, the way pre-order and level-order were for trees in Part III. Connectivity, articulation points, topological sort, strongly connected components are the structural questions you can ask in linear time over the graph. The middle of Part V is where the shortest-path canon lives — Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford, Floyd-Warshall, A\*-style heuristic search — and where the flow algorithms enter the book. The closing chapters cover NP-hard graph problems and the practical-vs-theoretical gap that defines the rest of the book.

The Part IV atoms keep paying out. KMP's failure function reappears in Aho-Corasick, which reappears in Part V as the basis for finite-state speech recognition. The suffix-array trick reappears as the FM-index for biological sequence alignment. The DP grid from chapter 30 generalizes to Part VI's entire toolkit. Hashing — chapter 12, then Rabin-Karp — reappears in Part VIII for geometric hashing and in Part IX for randomized graph algorithms.

Eight chapters, four questions, one part. Strings turned out to be where the book stops being about *operations on data* and starts being about *representations of structure*. Onward.
