# Codex Part V — Graphs: design

## Framing

Part V completes the June 2026 ship. Eight chapters plus a `lessons_5` capstone, written in Codex voice per `AGENT.md`: intuition before code, clean Python 3.13 reference implementations, back-of-envelope complexity, top-down decomposition for any algorithm whose extracted body exceeds ~20 lines.

The part presents graphs as the lifting of *one-dimensional* structures (sequences, hierarchies) to *arbitrary relations*. The trie at the end of Part IV was already a graph — the next eight chapters let the edges go anywhere. Chapters are ordered so each new algorithm can lean on a primitive from an earlier chapter without re-introducing it.

This part is independent of *Mostly Harmless Graphs* (sibling book in the Computist Library). MHG is problem-first, narrative-led, theorem-and-proof, framed around Graphtopia. Codex Part V is curriculum-aligned: one algorithm family per chapter, clean reference implementations, no cross-references to MHG. The two books address different reader contracts.

## Capstone — `lessons_5.qmd`

Themes to draw out (matches the depth of `lessons_4`):

- **Representation shapes algorithm.** Adjacency list vs. adjacency matrix isn't a storage choice — it changes which questions are cheap. Dijkstra's heap, MST's union-find, SCC's DFS stack: each algorithm picks a representation and the algorithm's cost shape falls out of it.
- **Relaxation as the unifying technique.** Dijkstra, Bellman-Ford, Floyd-Warshall, and Prim's MST are all *relaxation* algorithms — they maintain an upper-bound estimate and repeatedly tighten it. The difference is the order in which edges are considered. Once a reader sees this, half of graph algorithms look like one algorithm.
- **The cut property.** MST's correctness rests on a single graph-theoretic fact: every minimum-weight edge crossing any cut belongs to *some* MST. Kruskal and Prim are two reading orders of the same proof.
- **DFS is the swiss-army knife for structural questions.** Connectivity, cycle detection, topological ordering, SCC, bridge/articulation detection — all of them fall out of DFS plus a few bookkeeping tweaks (discovery times, finish times, low-link values).
- **Flow as duality made concrete.** Max-flow = min-cut isn't a coincidence; it's the LP-duality of the previous parts made fully visible. The augmenting-path algorithm is the constructive proof.

## Naming convention

Continues Parts I-IV: `<two-digit-number>_<lowercase_snake_case>.qmd`. The two-digit number is the global chapter number (so Part V is chapters 31-38).

## Implementation conventions (per `AGENT.md`)

- `docs/*.qmd` is the source of truth; `src/codex/graphs/*.py` and `tests/graphs/*.py` are generated by `make source`.
- Top-down decomposition: any algorithm whose body exceeds ~20 lines must be broken into smaller `def`s, each in its own `{export=...}` block with prose between. Named fragments (`{name=...}` + `<<...>>`) only when control flow crosses the boundary.
- All functions fully typed. Use `Sequence[T]` for inputs, modern generic syntax (`def func[T](...)`).
- For algorithms involving comparisons (Dijkstra's priority queue, MST), use the `Ordering[T]` protocol from `codex.types`.
- Every chapter's code blocks include both implementation and tests; `make tests` must pass green before the chapter is considered draft-complete.

## What this design does not specify

- Per-chapter outlines (sub-section headings, narrative beats, code-block sequence). Those come in the writing-plan stage, one per chapter.
- The specific examples and motivating problems each chapter opens with. The Codex style is intuition-first, so each chapter will pick one or two small worked examples; those are picked at draft time, not at design time.
- Exact page budget per chapter. Parts I-IV averaged ~12-15 pages per chapter; Part V should fall in the same range.
